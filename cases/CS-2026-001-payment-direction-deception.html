Payment-Direction Deception on a Classifieds Marketplace

1. Summary

A seller published a classifieds listing and was contacted by an unknown party within minutes. The contact sent two images — a QR-style graphic labelled as a ₹1.00 credit, and a photograph of a merchant POS terminal — then asked the seller to complete a step to "release" the money. Neither image evidenced an incoming payment. The seller checked the account balance rather than the screenshot, recognised the payment-direction reversal, and declined to act. No funds left the account.

The pattern matters because it needs no stolen credentials, no malware and no data breach. It needs one misread step: a victim who confuses receiving money with sending money.

2. Context

  • Who was exposed: an individual seller, not a business. The loss, had it succeeded, would have been the item's value plus whatever the "release" step demanded.
  • Platform: a large Indian classifieds marketplace where sellers publish a contact number or accept in-app chat, so the conversation moves off-platform almost immediately.
  • Timing: April 2026. The approach arrived within minutes of publication, consistent with automated monitoring of new listings rather than a human browsing the category.
  • Why it matters: card, UPI and wallet controls do not help here, because the victim authorises the transfer themselves.

3. What the parties saw

  1. Listing goes live; an unknown number opens contact on a messaging app.
  2. First image: a QR-style graphic carrying a "Received Money ₹1.00" label.
  3. Second image: a photograph of a merchant POS terminal showing a payment-processing interface.
  4. Message: the "buyer" says the payment is on hold and asks the seller to scan a code or open a link to receive it.

What was visible at the time was a plausible-looking pair of screenshots. What was established later is that neither artefact can evidence a credit: a QR graphic carries no transaction record, and a photograph of a terminal shows a device, not a settlement.

4. Method

  1. Preserve first. Capture the conversation with timestamps before replying to anything.
  2. Reconstruct the timeline. Log message timestamps at second-level granularity to test whether first contact was human or automated.
  3. Examine the artefacts. Image dimensions, compression and layout — whether the graphics match genuine app screenshots or are composites.
  4. Test the claim against the mechanism. Ask what record a genuine credit would produce — a bank or UPI reference, an app notification, a balance change — and check whether any of it exists. None did.
  5. Read the ask. Every payment-direction deception ends with an action that sends money or grants access. The ask is the tell, and it is the fastest thing to check.

6. Findings

  • Confirmed — contact was initiated within minutes of publication, before any negotiation.
  • Confirmed — neither artefact contained a transaction reference, and the account balance did not change.
  • Confirmed — the request that followed was an action that would have moved money outward.
  • Probable — the approach was templated and sent to multiple new listings; the wording and cadence were not tailored to the item.
  • Unconfirmed — who operated the number, and whether the POS photograph was genuine, borrowed or generated. No attribution is made.

7. Indicators and red flags

  • Payment "confirmed" by an image, never by a balance, notification or reference number.
  • Contact within minutes of publication.
  • Any request to scan a code, open a link, or enter a PIN in order to receive money.
  • A POS terminal or merchant-app screenshot offered as proof of a transfer to a personal account.
  • Pressure to act before the item is inspected or collected.
  • Move to a messaging app before the first real exchange.

8. What stopped it / what would have stopped it

  • The seller checked the balance instead of the screenshot. That single habit defeats the pattern.
  • A prompt on new listings — "never scan a code to receive money" — would remove most of the exposure at the point of contact.
  • In-app chat with number masking delays the off-platform move.

9. Limitations

  • Single case with no loss, so no loss figure is claimed.
  • The sender's identity was never established; nothing here should be read as an allegation against any identifiable person.
  • The judgement that the artefacts were composites rests on layout and metadata, not on device examination of the sender's phone.
