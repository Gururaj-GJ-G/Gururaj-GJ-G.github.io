What a Payment-Risk Reviewer Actually Wants From an Onboarding Pack

Abstract

Most merchant onboarding rejections are not judgements about a business model. They are the reviewer being unable to answer four questions from the documents in front of them: who is this, what do they sell, does the storefront support that claim, and where does the money go. This note sets out a five-layer assessment structure and an evidence-package template that answer those questions in the order a reviewer asks them. It is drawn from a compliance-first playbook prepared for a merchant-risk advisory engagement; client details are omitted.

1. The problem

A merchant submits a company registration, a bank statement and a website link. The reviewer has to decide, inside a service-level window, whether to approve, ask for more, or decline. When the pack is thin the only safe answer is "ask for more", and every round trip costs days, frustrates a legitimate merchant, and pushes the queue back.

The cost is asymmetric in a way that is worth stating plainly: a slow approval loses a customer; a wrong approval can cost the acquirer far more, in fines, scheme penalties and chargebacks. Reviewers behave accordingly, which is why vague packs get rejected rather than argued with.

2. Why current practice falls short

Three failure modes recur.

Documents without structure. Forty files with no cover note force the reviewer to reconstruct the story. Reconstructed stories get rejected.

Website claims without evidence. "We sell electronics" is a statement, not evidence. A storefront with three placeholder products, no returns policy and no business address contradicts the claim, and the contradiction is what the reviewer records.

Identity without linkage. The registered entity, the bank account holder and the payout beneficiary are frequently three different names. Unless the pack explains the relationship, it reads as concealment — which is precisely the pattern transaction-laundering controls exist to catch.

3. The proposed approach

Assess and document in five layers. Each layer answers one reviewer question and produces one section of the pack.

LayerQuestion it answersEvidence it produces
Identity and KYBWho is contracting, and who controls it?registration, UBO chain, director IDs, authorisation to sign
Business model and storefrontWhat is sold, to whom, at what price?live URLs, catalogue, pricing, shipping, returns, contact page
Category and product riskIs anything restricted, regulated or prohibited?product list with category mapping, licences where applicable
Early trading behaviourDoes actual traffic match the declared model?first-90-day volumes, average ticket, refund and chargeback rates
Payout exposure and linkageWhere does the money land, and who else lands there?settlement account proof, beneficiary linkage, shared-service disclosure

The order matters. Reviewers start at identity, and a pack that answers layers one and five but not two and three still gets returned.

4. How it works in practice

A worked shape, using a synthetic merchant:

  1. Cover note — one page: what the business does, monthly volume expected, ticket size, the three things about this merchant that usually raise questions and the answer to each.
  2. Identity — certificate of incorporation, UBO chart to natural persons, director identity, board resolution naming the signatory.
  3. Storefront — screenshots with URLs and capture dates, catalogue extract, returns and refund policy, customer-service contact that actually answers.
  4. Category — product list mapped to scheme and acquirer category rules, with the licence attached for anything regulated.
  5. Trading — the first-90-day plan, plus any historical processing data with the same descriptors the merchant will bill under.
  6. Payout — bank proof, a signed statement of the relationship between entity, account holder and beneficiary, and disclosure of any shared services with other merchants.

The single highest-value item is the descriptor in step six. A billing descriptor that does not match the storefront is the most common reason a clean merchant gets flagged, and the cheapest thing to fix before submission.

5. Trade-offs and failure modes

  • It costs the merchant real effort. A five-layer pack is not a two-click form; it is defensible precisely because it is work. Self-serve onboarding needs a lighter path with the same information collected progressively.
  • It cannot fix a bad business. A well-packaged prohibited merchant is still prohibited; the structure speeds the decline, it does not prevent it.
  • It decays. A pack is a snapshot. The layer that matters after month three is trading behaviour, which requires monitoring rather than documentation.
  • It over-documents small merchants if applied without proportion. Scale the evidence to the risk tier, not to the template.

6. What to measure

  • First-pass approval rate — share of applications approved without a document request.
  • Median round trips — document requests per application before decision.
  • Time to first transaction — submission to first settled payment.
  • Post-approval mismatch rate — approved merchants whose live descriptor or category differs from the declared one.
  • Early chargeback ratio at 90 days — the honest test of whether the pack predicted anything.

7. Conclusion

Build the pack around the reviewer's four questions, in their order, and put the descriptor at the front of your mind rather than the back. Most "merchant risk" problems at onboarding are evidence problems, and evidence problems are fixable before submission.
