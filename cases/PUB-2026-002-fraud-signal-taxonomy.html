A Fraud Signal Taxonomy That Survives an Audit

Abstract

Every fraud team accumulates signals. Few can say, on request, what a signal means, why it carries the weight it does, when it changed, or which decisions it has driven. That gap is what an auditor, regulator or challenger model finds first. This note proposes a taxonomy structure — seven categories, per-signal weight rationale, explicit trigger conditions, and a version stamp — that keeps a signal library defensible as it grows.

1. The problem

A signal that cannot be explained is a signal that cannot be defended. When a customer disputes a block, the question is not "was the model right?" but "what did you observe, and why did that justify the action?" A list of feature names answers neither part.

Three symptoms show the library has become folklore:

  • two teams implement "impossible travel" with different thresholds and both call it the same signal;
  • a weight is changed during an incident and nobody records it;
  • a signal is retired but still fires inside a legacy rule pack.

2. Why current practice falls short

Feature stores solve retrieval, not meaning. Model registries version models, not the semantics of the inputs. Neither tells an investigator that a signal means this device changed the SIM in the last twenty-four hours and the account changed its payout destination within the same window — which is the sentence that makes a decision defensible.

3. The proposed approach

Seven categories. Device, network, behaviour, authentication, account, linkage, transaction. The split is not academic: each category has a different evidence source, a different retention period and a different failure mode, so grouping by category makes it obvious where a library is thin. Most teams are heavy on transaction and device signals and almost empty on linkage — which is where mule and ring behaviour actually shows.

A record per signal. Every signal carries:

FieldWhy it exists
idstable key, never reused
nameplain-language meaning, no jargon
categoryone of the seven
triggerthe precise condition, in words an investigator can read
evidencewhat the investigator is shown when it fires
weightscore impact, plus the reasoning for that value
sourcesystem of record the signal is computed from
versionsemantic version of the definition
ownerthe team accountable for its accuracy

Named rule packs. Signals score; packs decide. A pack is a named combination mapped to a typology — account-takeover reset, impossible travel, emulator plus hooking, device farming, remote-control takeover, payout-abuse chains, mule pass-through. Naming them makes coverage auditable: you can say which typologies you detect and which you do not.

Deterministic outcomes. ALLOW, REVIEW or BLOCK, with the triggered signals, their evidence and a narrative. A deterministic engine will not out-perform a good model on raw detection; it will survive a conversation with a regulator, which is the binding constraint in most regulated environments.

4. How it works in practice

A decision narrative that an auditor can follow:

REVIEW — score 62. Triggered: DEV-SIM-CHANGE-01 (SIM changed 14 hours before the request; evidence: carrier record), ACC-PAYOUT-CHANGE-02 (payout destination added 11 hours before request), NET-ASN-NEW-03 (first sighting of this ASN for the account). Rule pack: payout-abuse chain. Not triggered: device compromise signals, so a takeover narrative is not supported — this is a payout-destination change, not a compromised handset.

Note what that narrative does: it states the decision, the signals, the evidence, and — critically — what was not observed and why that matters. The last part is what most systems omit and what investigators ask for.

5. Trade-offs and failure modes

  • Weights are opinions until calibrated. Write the rationale at the time of writing, not later; "0.35 because it appeared in 62% of confirmed mule cases in Q3" is defensible, "0.35" is not.
  • Deterministic engines do not generalise. They catch what you thought of. Pair them with monitoring for the typologies you have not named yet.
  • Taxonomies rot. Without an owner per signal and a review cadence, the library becomes a museum within a year.
  • Explainability has a cost. Narratives slow decisions slightly and require the evidence to be retrievable at decision time, which means retention policy has to be designed, not assumed.

6. What to measure

  • Signal coverage per category — how many typologies each category can support.
  • Firing rate per signal — a signal that never fires is dead weight; one that always fires is a broken threshold.
  • Narrative completeness — share of decisions where every triggered signal has attached evidence.
  • Override rate — how often investigators disagree with the engine, by pack. This is the best available feedback signal.
  • Definition churn — signals changed per quarter, and whether each change was recorded.

7. Conclusion

Write the library as if it will be read aloud in a dispute, because one day it will be. Seven categories, a reason for every weight, and a version on every definition cost a week and save the argument.
